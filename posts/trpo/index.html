Consider a infinite-horizon1 Markov decision process \(\mathcal M\) with states \(s \in \mathcal S\), actions \(a \in \mathcal A\), rewards \(r \in \mathbb R\), discount factor \(\gamma \in (0, 1)\), transition dynamics \(p(s', r \mid s, a)\), and initial state distribution \(\rho_0(s)\). In reinforcement learning, we learn a policy \(\pi \in \Pi\) that maximizes the expected cumulative discounted rewards \[ J(\pi) \mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits^{\text{def}}}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{s_0 \sim \rho_0} }\!\left[ V^{\pi}(s_0) \right]. \tag{1}\] While there are many methods to solve this, a popular approach is using policy gradient methods (see Sutton & Barto, chapter 13) which use gradient descent to directly optimize \(J(\pi)\) using \(\nabla_\boldsymbol\theta J(\pi)\), where the policy \(\pi(a \mid s)\) is parametized by some vector \(\boldsymbol\theta\in \mathbb R^d\) (e.g. a neural network).

1 While the analysis is more difficult in the finite-horizon case, the results are the same.

Example: Common Policy Spaces

One common policy space for discrete state and action spaces is the Boltzmann policy (or softmax policy) parameterized by \(\boldsymbol\theta\in \mathbb R^{|\mathcal S| \times |\mathcal A|}\). It’s defined for \(\pi \in \Pi_\text{boltzmann}\) as \[ \pi(a \mid s) = \frac{\exp(\theta_{s, a})}{\sum_{a' \in \mathcal A} \theta_{s, a'}}. \]

For continuous state and action spaces, a common method is to use neural networks \(\boldsymbol\mu_\boldsymbol\theta\) and \(\boldsymbol\sigma_\boldsymbol\theta\) to predict the mean and covariance of a normal distribution. It’s defined for \(\pi \in \Pi_\text{neural\_network}\) as \[ \pi(a \mid s) = \mathcal{N}\big(\boldsymbol\mu_\boldsymbol\theta(s), \text{diag}(\boldsymbol\sigma_\boldsymbol\theta(s))\big). \]

1 Policy Gradient Methods

One of the first policy gradient methods is REINFORCE, which uses the policy gradient theorem \[ \nabla_\boldsymbol\theta J(\pi_\boldsymbol\theta) = \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\tau \sim \pi_\boldsymbol\theta} }\!\left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\big( R_t(\tau) - b(s_t) \big) \nabla_\boldsymbol\theta\log \pi_\boldsymbol\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \right]. \] to update \(\boldsymbol\theta\) (where \(R_t(\tau)\) is the reward-to-go \(\sum_{k=t}^T \gamma^{k - t}r_k\) and \(b:\mathcal S\to\mathbb R\) is some baseline function). To evaluate this, it takes a single Monte Carlo sample \(\tau = (s_0, a_0, r_0, \dots, s_T, a_T, r_T)\) from \(\pi_\boldsymbol\theta\), forming the approximation \[ \widetilde\nabla_\boldsymbol\theta J(\pi_\boldsymbol\theta) = \sum_{t=0}^\infty\big( R_t(\tau) - b(s_t) \big) \nabla_\boldsymbol\theta\log \pi_\boldsymbol\theta(a_t \mid s_t). \] While this is a unbiased estimator, it has unbounded variance. Following the analysis in this blog post by Daniel Seita we can approximate the variance of the gradient estimate as \[ \operatorname*{cov}_{ \substack{\tau \sim \pi_\boldsymbol\theta} }\!\left[ \widetilde\nabla_\boldsymbol\theta J(\pi_\boldsymbol\theta) \right] \approx \sum_{t=0}^\infty\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\tau \sim \pi_\boldsymbol\theta} }\!\left[ \frac{\nabla_\boldsymbol\theta\log \pi_\boldsymbol\theta(a_t \mid s_t)^\top\nabla_\boldsymbol\theta\log \pi_\boldsymbol\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_\boldsymbol\theta(a_t \mid s_t)^2} \right] \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\tau \sim \pi_\boldsymbol\theta} }\!\left[ \vphantom{\sum}\big( R_t(\tau) - b(s_t) \big)^2 \right]. \] While the magnitude of the second term can be reduced using contral variates to set the baseline \(b\) as an approximation of the value function, the first term is not so easy to deal with. Importantly, its magnitude scales roughly with \(\pi_\boldsymbol\theta(a_t \mid s_t)^{-2}\), which grows very large for low-probability actions and large trajectories. The only easy way to reduce this variance is to take more Monte Carlo rollouts, which can be prohibativly expensive.

The high variance of this updates is the major drawback of REINFORCE since we have no guarantee that the optimizer is moving in the right direction, i.e. it’s easy for the expected cumulative discounted rewards to get worse during updates due to unlucky Monte Carlo rollouts. This raises the question of is there a different update step we can use that avoids taking bad update steps?

2 Trust Region Policy Optimization

Taking inspiration from classical optimization algorithms, one approach is to consider a trust region method, where we have some radius within which we “trust” our approximation of the objective function. This would be helpful here, since if we have a high variance step, it would move us far away from our current location, which would be outside of the trust region. However, the difficulty with implementing this comes from the fact that we don’t want to have to sample from the new policy to calculate this distance (if we were able to that efficiently, then we wouldn’t even need to modify REINFORCE—we could just reject updates if they’re too far away). The first natural question to ask is then can we evaluate the performance of the new policy in terms of the old policy?

This is the main idea of trust region policy optimization (TRPO). All of the following derivations, starting with the surrogate advantage objective function going to the Taylor approximation of the Hessian will all come down to this core idea.

2.1 Deriving the Surrogate Advantage

Consider an update moving from policy \(\pi_\text{old}\) to policy \(\pi\). If we want to ensure the optimizer takes a good step, we need to evaluate \(J(\pi)\) and check that it’s greater than \(J(\pi_\text{old})\). However, this is exceedingly expensive, which motivates the question of can we evaluate the performance of \(\pi\) using only \(\pi_\text{old}\)? To do this, we’ll define a new objective function called the surrogate advantage.

Starting with the expected cumulative discounted rewards \(J(\pi)\), we can replace \(r_t\) with \(A^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_t, a_t)\): \[ \begin{alignat*}{2} J(\pi) &= \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{s_0 \sim \rho_0} }\!\left[ V^{\pi}(s_0) \right] \\ &= \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\pi} }\!\left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t r_t \right] && \text{(definition of $V^{\pi}$)} \\ &= \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\pi} }\!\left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t\big( r_t + \textcolor{WildStrawberry}{ V^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_{t+1}) - V^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_{t+1}) } \big) \right] \\ &= \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\pi} }\!\left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t\big( \textcolor{WildStrawberry}{ Q^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_t, a_t) } - V^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_{t+1}) \big) \right] && \text{(definition of $Q^{\pi_\text{old}}$)} \\ &= \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\pi} }\!\left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t \big(\textcolor{WildStrawberry}{ A^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_t, a_t) + V^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_t) } - V^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_{t+1})\big) \right] \qquad && \text{(definition of $A^{\pi_\text{old}}$)} \\ &= \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\pi} }\!\left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t A^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_t, a_t) \right] + \textcolor{WildStrawberry}{ \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{s_0 \sim \rho_0} }\!\left[ V^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_0) \right] } && \text{(telescoping sum)} \\ &= \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\pi} }\!\left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t A^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_t, a_t) \right] + J(\pi_\text{old}). \end{alignat*} \] This result is known as the performance difference lemma: \[ \mathcal L(\pi, \pi_\text{old}) \mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits^{\text{def}}}J(\pi) - J(\pi_\text{old}) = \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\pi} }\!\left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^tA^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_t, a_t) \right]. \] Since \(J(\pi_\text{old})\) is independent of \(\pi\), we can optimize \(\mathcal L(\pi, \pi_\text{old})\) instead of \(J(\pi)\) to find the optimal policy. Importantly, we see that we only need to evaluate the advantage of \(\pi_\text{old}\) instead of \(\pi\)!

However, to calculate the expectation we still need Monte Carlo rollouts from \(\pi\) which is inefficient. To manipulate the expectation, it’s easier to consider individual states as opposed to entire trajectories. To this end we introduce the state distribution \[ \rho_\pi(s) \mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits^{\text{def}}}(1 - \gamma) \sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t \Pr(s_t = s \mid s_0 \sim \rho_0, \pi) \] and rewrite the performance difference2 as \[ \mathcal L(\pi, \pi_\text{old}) = \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{s \sim \rho_\pi \\ a \sim \pi(\cdot \mid s)} }\!\left[ A^{\pi_\text{old}}(s, a) \right]. \] In this formulation, there are two distributions being sampled from in the expectation: \(\rho_\pi\) and \(\pi(\cdot \mid s)\).

2 If it’s not clear why this is possible, there are only two operations happening here: we introduce and distribute the fraction \(\frac{1 - \gamma}{1 - \gamma}\), and we move the policy from the expectation to the probability term in \(\rho_\pi\). The logic is similar to that of the policy gradient theorem proof presented in Sutton & Barto chapter 13.

3 We assume that \(\pi\) and \(\pi_\text{old}\) are both well-behaved, i.e. \(\pi(a \mid s) \neq 0\) for all \(a \in \mathcal A\) and \(s \in \mathcal S\).

Of these, \(\pi(\cdot \mid s)\) has a closed-form, e.g. a neural network. Forward passes in neural networks are efficient to compute, so we can use importance sampling to sample from \(\pi_\text{old}(\cdot \mid s)\) instead3: \[ \mathcal L(\pi, \pi_\text{old}) = \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{s \sim \rho_\pi \\ \textcolor{WildStrawberry}{ a \sim \pi_\text{old}(\cdot \mid s) }} }\!\left[ \textcolor{WildStrawberry}{ \frac{\pi(a \mid s)}{\pi_\text{old}(a \mid s)} } A^{\pi_\text{old}}(s,a) \right]. \]

Unfortunately, \(\rho_\pi\) has no closed-form solution meaning we can’t use importance sampling to sample from \(\rho_{\pi_\text{old}}\).

If we consider how policy gradient methods work, they generally take iterative steps leading to a sequence of policies \(\{\pi_1, \pi_2, \dots, \pi_k\}\). Intuitively, each consecutive policy should be decently similar, i.e. \(\pi \approx \pi_\text{old}\). If we make that assumption, then it follows that \(\rho_\pi \approx \rho_{\pi_\text{old}}\). If we replace \(\rho_\pi\) with \(\rho_{\pi_\text{old}}\) in the performance difference function, then we get the surrogate advantage objective \[ \widetilde{\mathcal L}(\pi, \pi_\text{old}) = \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\textcolor{WildStrawberry}{ s \sim \rho_{\pi_\text{old}} } \\ a \sim \pi_\text{old}(\cdot \mid s)} }\!\left[ \frac{\pi(a \mid s)}{\pi_\text{old}(a \mid s)} A^{\pi_\text{old}}(s,a) \right]. \] In the surrogate advantage, our expectation is over exclusively \(\pi_\text{old}\), meaning we can reuse Monte Carlo rollouts of \(\pi_\text{old}\) without simulating \(\pi\). Additionally, so long as \(\pi \approx \pi_\text{old}\) we know that \(J(\pi) \approx \widetilde{\mathcal L}(\pi, \pi_\text{old}) + J(\pi_\text{old})\), meaning that we can estimate \(J(\pi)\) while only using \(\pi_\text{old}\). This is exactly what we wanted from our new objective!

2.2 The Monotone Convergence Guarantee

From Section 2.1, we know that \(w\mathcal L(\pi, \pi_\text{old}) \approx \mathcal L(\pi, \pi_\text{old})\) only if \(\pi \approx \pi_\text{old}\), so we need some way to formalize when this is true. The standard way to measure the similarity of probability distributions is the Kullback-Leibler divergence \[ D_\text{KL}^{} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old}(\cdot \mid s) \,\|\, \pi(\cdot \mid s) \vphantom{\big(} \right) \mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits^{\text{def}}}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{a \sim \pi_\text{old}(\cdot \mid s)} }\!\left[ \log\left(\frac{\pi_\text{old}(a \mid s)}{\pi(a \mid s)}\right) \right]. \]

However, the policy can change for each state, so we need a way to aggregate the KL divergence into a single measurement. Mathematically, it’s easiest to use the worst-case divergence \[ D_\text{KL}^{\max} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right) = \max_{s \in \mathcal S} D_\text{KL}^{} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old}(\cdot \mid s) \,\|\, \pi(\cdot \mid s) \vphantom{\big(} \right). \] Intuitively, this states that even if \(\pi\) and \(\pi_\text{old}\) normally take the same action, if there’s even one state where they differ that will be reflected in \(D_\text{KL}^{\max} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right)\). This makes sense for reinforcement learning, where there’s often a few critical states (e.g. a cliff that the agent could fall off of), and we may not be sure which states those are.

Using the worst-case KL divergence, the paper Trust Region Policy Optimization by Schulman et. al. proves the monotone convergence theorem:

Monotone Convergence Theorem

Theorem 1 (Monotone Convergence) For two policies \(\pi, \pi_\text{old}\in \Pi\) we have that \[ \mathcal L(\pi, \pi_\text{old}) \geq \widetilde{\mathcal L}(\pi, \pi_\text{old}) - CD_\text{KL}^{\max} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right), \] where \(C\) is a function of the discount factor \(\gamma\) and the maximum advantage of \(\pi_\text{old}\).

This means that if we use the update step \[ \pi_\text{new}\gets \mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\pi \in \Pi}\; \widetilde{\mathcal L}(\pi, \pi_\text{old}) - CD_\text{KL}^{\max} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right) \tag{2}\] we will always converge to the optimal policy since Equation 2 is bounded by zero when \(\pi=\pi_\text{old}\), i.e. it must be true that \(J(\pi_0) \leq J(\pi_1) \leq \cdots \leq J(\pi_k)\).

This theorem clearly fixes the issue of knowing when \(\pi \approx \pi_\text{old}\), since the \(CD_\text{KL}^{\max} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right)\) acts as a penalty parameter, reducing our improvement as we get further from \(\pi_\text{old}\). However, this also fixes the original policy gradient problem of high variance! Since we now control how far away \(\pi\) and \(\pi_\text{old}\) are, we can be confident that our step is a good one!

This is why the penalty term is the worst-case KL divergence, since it measures the distance in policy space and not the parameter space. If we were to instead use a parameter space measure like the \(\ell_2\) norm \(\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol\theta- \boldsymbol\theta_\text{old}\|^2\), the resulting policies could still be different since they’re non-linear in \(\boldsymbol\theta\). In essence, \(\boldsymbol\theta\approx \boldsymbol\theta_\text{old}\) does not imply that \(\pi \approx \pi_\text{old}\), which was causing the high variance of the policy gradient updates!

However, while this update is theoretically sound it’s difficult to implement. The first issue is that since the \(C\) parameter depends on the maximum advantage of \(\pi_\text{old}\), it’s intractable to calculate for large or unbounded state spaces. Ideally, we could set \(C\) as a global hyperparameter, but this proves to be difficult. Intuitively, this is because \(C\) directly affects both the divergence term and the surrogate advantage term. For one update, a value of \(C = 10^{-3}\) could be large relative to the surrogate advantage, and for another it could be small, even if the worst-case divergence is the same.

In practice, we rewrite Equation 2 as an equivalent dual constrained optimization problem \[ \pi_\text{new}\gets \mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\pi \in \Pi}\; \widetilde{\mathcal L}(\pi, \pi_\text{old}) \text{ such that }D_\text{KL}^{\max} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right) \leq \delta, \tag{3}\] which replaces the \(C\) parameter with a \(\delta\) parameter that also depends on \(\gamma\) and the maximum advantage. However, because the \(\delta\) parameter is exclusively in the constraint it’s easier to set as a global hyperparameter. Intuitively, since it doesn’t impact the surrogate advantage it will always directly bound the worst-case divergence the same, no matter where in policy space you are.

2.3 Quadratic Trust Region Optimization

Unfortunately, it’s not clear how to actually solve for the update in Equation 3. Theoretically, there are closed form solutions for constrained optimization problems with linear objectives and quadratic constraints, so if we approximate \(\widetilde{\mathcal L}(\pi, \pi_\text{old})\) with a linear function and \(D_\text{KL}^{\max} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right)\) with a quadratic function we can use the closed-form update. Additionally, at each update we’ve calculated and simulated \(\pi_\text{old}\), so we have access to Monte Carlo rollouts and an advantage estimate. Since we’re only considering policies \(\pi\) that are close to \(\pi_\text{old}\) due to the worst-case divergence constraint, we can use Taylor series approximations to estimate \(\widetilde{\mathcal L}(\pi, \pi_\text{old})\) and \(D_\text{KL}^{\max} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right)\).

2.3.1 Approximating \(D_\text{KL}^{\max} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right)\)

Since \(D_\text{KL}^{\max} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right)\) is a maximum over the state space, we need to enumerate every element which is intractable for large or unbounded state spaces. In policy gradient methods, we generally interact with the environment using Monte Carlo rollouts, so let’s instead consider the expected divergence \[ \overline D_\text{KL}^{} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right) \mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits^{\text{def}}}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{s \sim \rho_{\pi_\text{old}}} }\!\left[ D_\text{KL}^{} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old}(\cdot \mid s) \,\|\, \pi(\cdot \mid s) \vphantom{\big(} \right) \right]. \] If we approximate \(D_\text{KL}^{\max} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right)\) with \(\overline D_\text{KL}^{} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right)\) then it’s possible that this misses some key states with high divergence, but overall it will accomplish the same result of stopping the new policy from straying too far from the old policy.

The second-order Taylor approximation of \(\overline D_\text{KL}^{} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right)\) centered at \(\pi=\pi_\text{old}\) is \[ \begin{align*} \overline D_\text{KL}^{} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right) &\approx \left[\overline D_\text{KL}^{} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right)\right]_{\pi=\pi_\text{old}} \\ &\quad +\; (\boldsymbol\theta- \boldsymbol\theta_\text{old})^\top \nabla_\boldsymbol\theta\left[\overline D_\text{KL}^{} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right)\right]_{\pi = \pi_\text{old}} \\ &\quad +\; \frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol\theta- \boldsymbol\theta_\text{old})^\top\nabla_\boldsymbol\theta^2\left[\overline D_\text{KL}^{} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right)\right]_{\pi = \pi_\text{old}}(\boldsymbol\theta- \boldsymbol\theta_\text{old}) \end{align*} \] It’s clear that \(\overline D_\text{KL}^{} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi_\text{old} \vphantom{\big(} \right) = 0\), which drops out. Additionally, some algebra shows that \(\nabla_\boldsymbol\theta\left[\overline D_\text{KL}^{} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right)\right]_{\pi=\pi_\text{old}} = \mathbf 0\), causing the first-order term to drop out as well. Thus, the second-order approximation is completely determined by the Hessian matrix \[ \mathbf H= \nabla_\boldsymbol\theta^2\left[\overline D_\text{KL}^{} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right)\right]_{\pi=\pi_\text{old}} = -\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{s \sim \rho_{\pi_\text{old}} \\ a \sim \pi_\text{old}(\cdot \mid s)} }\!\left[ \nabla_\boldsymbol\theta^2 \log \pi(a \mid s) \right]. \] Thus, our final approximation is \[ \overline D_\text{KL}^{} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right) \approx \frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol\theta- \boldsymbol\theta_\text{old})^\top \mathbf H(\boldsymbol\theta- \boldsymbol\theta_\text{old}). \tag{4}\]

2.3.2 Approximating \(\widetilde{\mathcal L}(\pi, \pi_\text{old})\)

The first-order Taylor expansion centered at \(\pi=\pi_\text{old}\) (ignoring the constant term since it doesn’t afffect optimization) is \[ \begin{align*} \mathbf g&= \nabla_\boldsymbol\theta\widetilde{\mathcal L}(\pi, \pi_\text{old}) \\ &= \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{s \sim \rho_{\pi_\text{old}} \\ a \sim \pi_\text{old}(\cdot \mid s)} }\!\left[ \nabla_\boldsymbol\theta\frac{\pi(a \mid s)}{\pi_\text{old}(a \mid s)} A^{\pi_\text{old}}(s, a) \right] \\ &= \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{s \sim \rho_{\pi_\text{old}} \\ a \sim \pi_\text{old}(\cdot \mid s)} }\!\left[ A^{\pi_\text{old}}(s, a) \nabla_\boldsymbol\theta\log \pi(a \mid s) \right]. \end{align*} \] This is actually the same as \(\nabla_\boldsymbol\theta J(\pi_\text{old})\) from the policy gradient theorem (see Sutton & Barto, chapter 13), providing further justification that \(\widetilde{\mathcal L}(\pi, \pi_\text{old})\) is a good stand-in for \(J(\pi)\). Our final estimate is then \[ \widetilde{\mathcal L}(\pi, \pi_\text{old}) \approx (\boldsymbol\theta- \boldsymbol\theta_\text{old})^\top\mathbf g. \tag{5}\]

2.3.3 Solving the Update Step

How do we efficiently calculate the inverse of the Hessian?

Calculating \(\mathbf H^{-1}\mathbf g\) is normally \(\mathcal O(d^3)\) time. Instead, since the Hessian is the expected Fisher information matrix of the policy we can implement Hessian products as the operator \[ \mathbf H\mathbf x= \nabla_\boldsymbol\theta\!\left[ \left(\nabla_\boldsymbol\theta\overline D_\text{KL}^{} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right)\right)^\top\mathbf x\right]. \] Using a Krylov subspace method like conjugate gradient, we can use this to calculate \(\mathbf H^{-1}\mathbf g\) with a time complexity of roughly \(\mathcal O\!\left(d\sqrt{\kappa(\mathbf H)}\right)\). Importantly, this only requires access to \(\nabla_\boldsymbol\theta\log \pi(a \mid s)\), which is easy to calculate for even complex policies using automatic differentiation.

Using the approximations Equation 5 and Equation 4, we form the update step \[ \boldsymbol\theta_\text{new}\gets \mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\boldsymbol\theta\in \mathbb R^d}\, \mathbf g^\top(\boldsymbol\theta- \boldsymbol\theta_\text{old}) \text{ such that }\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol\theta- \boldsymbol\theta_\text{old})^\top\mathbf H(\boldsymbol\theta- \boldsymbol\theta_\text{old}) \leq \delta \] which has the closed-form solution \[ \boldsymbol\theta_\text{new}\gets \boldsymbol\theta_\text{old}+ \left(\sqrt{\frac{2 \delta}{\mathbf g^\top\mathbf H^{-1}\mathbf g}}\right) \mathbf H^{-1}\mathbf g \] Interestingly, this has the form of a natural gradient update, which is similar to a standard gradient descent update but accounts for the curvature of the policy space. However, since we approximated \(\overline D_\text{KL}^{} \!\left( \vphantom{\big(} \pi_\text{old} \,\|\, \pi \vphantom{\big(} \right)\), it’s possible that \(\boldsymbol\theta_\text{new}\) may not satisfy the constraint. Luckily this is simple to fix with a backtracking line search (see Nocedal & Wright chapter 3), where we introduce some \(\alpha \in (0, 1]\) and choose the largest \(\alpha\) such that \[ \boldsymbol\theta_\text{new}\gets \boldsymbol\theta_\text{old}+ \alpha\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{2 \delta}{\mathbf g^\top\mathbf H^{-1}\mathbf g}}\right) \mathbf H^{-1}\mathbf g \tag{6}\] satisfies the expected divergence constraint. Now we have an update that we can efficiently calculate that approximately satisfies Theorem 1!

2.4 What Have we Accomplished?

We now have Equation 6 which we can solve efficiently by only evaluating and sampling from \(\pi_\text{old}\). This update is guaranteed to satisfy the expected divergence constraint in Equation 3 due to the backtracking line search, but uses a first-order approximation (Equation 5) of the surrogate advantage. Assuming that this approximation is good enough, the monotone convergence theorem (Theorem 1) guarantees that this update will converge, fixing our issue with vanilla policy gradients!

3 Proximal Policy Optimization

While TRPO has nice theoretical guarantees, the complicated update step means it can be slow in practice. Proximal policy optimization (PPO) fixes this by replacing the divergence constraint in Equation 3 with a much simpler clipping process.

It minimizes the objective \[ \mathcal L_\text{PPO}(\boldsymbol\theta) = \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{t} }\!\left[ A^{\pi_\text{old}}(s, a)\min\left\{\frac{\pi(a \mid s)}{\pi_\text{old}(a \mid s)}, \text{clip}\left(\frac{\pi(a \mid s)}{\pi_\text{old}(a \mid s)}, 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon\right)\right\} \right] \] directly with a first order optimizer like stochastic gradient descent or ADAM.
