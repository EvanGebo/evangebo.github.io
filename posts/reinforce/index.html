In reinforcement learning, we seek to learn a policy \(\pi\) that maximizes the expected discounted rewards \[ J(\pi) \mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits^{\text{def}}}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{s_0 \sim \rho_0} }\!\left[ V^{\pi}(s_0) \right]. \] There are many algorithms to solve this, and a common approach in modern methods is policy gradient methods, where we parameterize our policy \(\pi_\boldsymbol\theta\) in terms of a parameter vector \(\boldsymbol\theta\in \mathbb R^d\), and then optimize \(J(\pi_\boldsymbol\theta)\) using the gradient \(\nabla_\boldsymbol\theta J(\pi_\boldsymbol\theta)\).

Policy Gradient Methods

In policy gradient methods, we use Monte Carlo sampling to sample trajectories \(\tau = ( s_0, a_0, r_0, \dots, s_T, a_T, r_T )\) from a policy \(\pi\). A classic algorithm is REINFORCE, which uses the identity \[ \nabla_\boldsymbol\theta J(\pi_\boldsymbol\theta) = \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\tau \sim \pi_\boldsymbol\theta} }\!\left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\big( R_t(\tau) - b(s_t) \big) \nabla_\boldsymbol\theta\log \pi_\boldsymbol\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \right]. \] To update the parameters \(\boldsymbol\theta\), REINFORCE approximates \(\nabla J(\boldsymbol\theta)\) with a single Monte Carlo sample \(\tau \sim \pi_\boldsymbol\theta\): \[ \widetilde\nabla_\boldsymbol\theta J(\pi_\boldsymbol\theta) = \sum_{t=0}^\infty\big( R_t(\tau) - b(s_t) \big) \nabla_\boldsymbol\theta\log \pi_\boldsymbol\theta(a_t \mid s_t). \]

While this is a unbiased estimator, it has unbounded variance. Following the analysis in [1] we see that \[ \operatorname*{cov}_{ \substack{\tau \sim \pi_\boldsymbol\theta} }\!\left[ \widetilde\nabla_\boldsymbol\theta J(\pi_\boldsymbol\theta) \right] \approx \sum_{t=0}^\infty\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\tau \sim \pi_\boldsymbol\theta} }\!\left[ \frac{\nabla_\boldsymbol\theta\log \pi_\boldsymbol\theta(a_t \mid s_t)^\top\nabla_\boldsymbol\theta\log \pi_\boldsymbol\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_\boldsymbol\theta(a_t \mid s_t)^2} \right] \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\tau \sim \pi_\boldsymbol\theta} }\!\left[ \vphantom{\sum}\big( R_t(\tau) - V^{\pi_\boldsymbol\theta}(s_t) \big)^2 \right]. \] The first term will scale with \(\pi_\boldsymbol\theta(a_t \mid s_t)^{-2}\), which grows very large for low-probability actions and large trajectories. The only way to reduce this variance is by taking more Monte Carlo samples, which can be prohibitevly expensive.

High variance is a large issue, since it means that we have no guarantee that our optimizer is moving in the right direction, i.e. it’s very easy for us to get worse steps when we update, due to bad Monte Carlo samples. This raises the question of can we change our update formula such that we are more likely to improve?

Trust Region Policy Optimization

Taking inspiration from classical optimization algorithms, one approach is to consider a trust region method, where we have some radius within which we “trust” our approximation of the objective function. This would be helpful here, since if we have a high variance step, it would move us far away from our current location, which would be outside the trust region. However, the difficulty with implementing this comes from the fact that we don’t want to have to sample from the new policy (if we were fine with that, we could just stick to REINFORCE). The first natural question to ask then, is can we evaluate the performance of the new policy in terms of the old policy? This is one of the fundamental ideas of trust region policy optimization (TRPO) and leads to the definition of the surrogate advantage objective function.

Deriving the Surrogate Advantage

If we want to ensure that we take good steps, we need to evaluate the policy. However, if we want to be efficient, we want to avoid actually sampling from that policy. This raises the question of can we express the performance of one policy in terms of another policy? This is the question that motivates the derivation of TRPO’s objective function: surrogate advantage.

Using the advantage function, linearity of expectation, and the law of total expectation, we can replace \(r_t\) with \(A^{\pi}(s_t, a_t)\) for any arbitrary policy \(\pi\): \[ \begin{align*} J(\pi) &= \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{s_0 \sim \rho_0} }\!\left[ V^{\pi}(s_0) \right] \\ &= \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\pi} }\!\left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t r_t \right] \\ &= \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\pi} }\!\left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t\big( \underbrace{r_t + V^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_{t+1})}_{Q^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_t, a_t)} - V^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_{t+1}) \big) \right] \\ &= \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\pi} }\!\left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t\big( \underbrace{Q^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_t, a_t)}_{A^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_t, a_t) + V^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_t)} - V^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_{t+1}) \big) \right] \\ &= \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\pi} }\!\left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t \big(A^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_t, a_t) + \underbrace{V^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_t) - V^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_{t+1})}_{\text{cancels to }V^{\pi_\text{old}}{(s_0)}}\big) \right] \\ &= \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\pi} }\!\left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t A^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_t, a_t) \right] + \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{s_0 \sim \rho_0} }\!\left[ V^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_0) \right] \\ &= \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\pi} }\!\left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t A^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_t, a_t) \right] + J(\pi_\text{old}). \end{align*} \] This result is known as the performance difference theorem, since it lets us define the performance difference \(\mathcal L\) of a policy \(\pi\) compared to another policy \(\pi_\text{old}\): \[ \mathcal L(\pi; \pi_\text{old}) \mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits^{\text{def}}}J(\pi) - J(\pi_\text{old}) = \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\pi} }\!\left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^tA^{\pi_\text{old}}(s_t, a_t) \right] \] Since \(J(\pi_\text{old})\) is independent of \(\pi\), we can easily optimize for \(\mathcal L(\pi; \pi_\text{old})\) instead of \(J(\pi)\) to find the optimal policy. Importantly, we see that we only need to evaluate the advantage policy of the old policy instead of the new policy!

However, we still need to sample trajectory rollouts from \(\pi\), which doesn’t fix our efficiency issues since to determine whether the update is good or not, we need to rollout sample tgrajectoriers from \(\pi\). To fix this, we’ll have to write the expectation in a different way. It’s difficult to consider trajectories as a whole, but it’s much easier to consider the general distribution of states, i.e. where the agent spends its time. We can formalize this with the state distribution \(\rho_\pi\): \[ \rho_\pi(s) \mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits^{\text{def}}}(1 - \gamma) \sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t \Pr(s_t = s \mid s_0 \sim \rho_0, \pi). \] Using this distribution, we can rewrite the performance difference1 as \[ \mathcal L(\pi; \pi_\text{old}) = \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{s \sim \rho_\pi \\ a \sim \pi(\cdot \mid s)} }\!\left[ A^{\pi_\text{old}}(s, a) \right]. \]

Importantly, \(\pi(\cdot \mid s)\) has a user-specified closed-form, e.g. a neural network. Since a forward pass is easy to compute (it’s the transition dynamcis that are computationally expensive to simulate) we can use importance sampling to sample from \(\pi_\text{old}(\cdot \mid s)\) instead: \[ \mathcal L(\pi; \pi_\text{old}) = \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{s \sim \rho_\pi \\ \textcolor{WildStrawberry}{ a \sim \pi_\text{old}(\cdot \mid s) }} }\!\left[ \textcolor{WildStrawberry}{ \frac{\pi(a \mid s)}{\pi_\text{old}(a \mid s)} } A^{\pi_\text{old}}(s,a) \right]. \]

Unfortanantly, since \(\rho_\pi\) has no closed-form solution we can’t use importance sampling to sample from \(\rho_{\pi_\text{old}}\), and due to the complexity of the state distribution there are no fancy tricks we can use to rewrite this. So what have we gained be rewriting the optimization problem in terms of \(\mathcal L(\pi; \pi_\text{old})\) instead of \(J(\pi)\)? Two important things:

  1. We now only need to evaluate the advantage of \(\pi_\text{old}\) instead of \(\pi\), which means we don’t need to train a function approximator \(A^{\pi}_\boldsymbol\phi\) for \(A^{\pi}\) (as is required in actor-critic methods), we only need to approximate \(A^{\pi_\text{old}}\).
  2. Instead of sampling from a set of distributions \(\pi(\cdot \mid s_t)\) and \(p(\cdot, \cdot \mid s_t, a_t)\), we sample from a single distribution \(\rho_\pi\).

The second point here is important, since even though we can’t find a closed-form solution to do this sampling, it does let us more efficiently analyze the samples. The main result is the monotone improvement guarantee, which states that if \(\rho_\pi\) and \(\rho_{\pi_\text{old}}\) are close enough, then we can just sample from \(\rho_{\pi_\text{old}}\) instead. This idea of just replacing that sample leads to the surrogate advantage objective function \(\widetilde{\mathcal L}\):

\[ \widetilde{\mathcal L}(\pi; \pi_\text{old}) = \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{\textcolor{WildStrawberry}{ s \sim \rho_{\pi_\text{old}} } \\ a \sim \pi_\text{old}(\cdot \mid s)} }\!\left[ \frac{\pi(a \mid s)}{\pi_\text{old}(a \mid s)} A^{\pi_\text{old}}(s, a) \right]. \]

Primarily, TRPO uses the monotone convergence guarantee2, which states that there exists some \(\delta \in \mathbb R_{\geq 0}\) such that so long as \[ \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{s \sim \rho_{\pi_\text{old}}} }\!\left[ D_\text{KL}\left( \vphantom{\sum}\pi_\text{old}(\cdot \mid s) \;\middle\|\; \pi(\cdot \mid s) \right) \right] \leq \delta, \] \(\pi\) is guaranteed to be better than \(\pi_\text{old}\), i.e. \(\mathcal L(\pi; \pi_\text{old}) \geq 0\) or equivilently \(J(\pi) \geq J(\pi_\text{old})\). For convienience, going forward I’ll denote \[ \overline{D}_\text{KL}( \pi_\text{old} \;\|\; \pi ) = \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{ \substack{s \sim \rho_{\pi_\text{old}}} }\!\left[ D_\text{KL}\left( \vphantom{\sum}\pi_\text{old}(\cdot \mid s) \;\middle\|\; \pi(\cdot \mid s) \right) \right]. \]

This result gives us exactly what we wanted: a way to guarantee policy improvement (thus avoiding the high-variance of the REINFORCE estimator) without evaluating the new policy (fixing the sample inneficiency issues). However, this formulation does have some issues of its own, since it’s not clear how to solve this update step: \[ \begin{align*} \boldsymbol\theta_{k+1} \gets \mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\boldsymbol\theta\in \mathbb R^d}\;& \widetilde{\mathcal L}(\pi_\boldsymbol\theta; \pi_{\boldsymbol\theta_k}) \\ \text{s.t. }&\overline{D}_\text{KL}( \pi_{\boldsymbol\theta_k} \;\|\; \pi_\boldsymbol\theta) \leq \delta. \end{align*} \]

Quadratic Trust Region Optimization

The update step does have a form similar to other problems, namely a trust region update step. There are existing algorithms to solve these using quadratic Taylor expansions, i.e. using \[ \begin{align*} \mathbf g_k &= \nabla_\boldsymbol\theta\!\left[ \widetilde{L}(\pi_\boldsymbol\theta, \pi_{\boldsymbol\theta_k}) \right] \\ \mathbf H_k &= \nabla_\boldsymbol\theta^2 \!\left[ \overline{D}_\text{KL}( \pi_\text{old} \;\|\; \pi ) \right]. \end{align*} \] I won’t go through the specifics, but in essence we can solve this quadratic approximation for an approximate step [TODO]. We can then use a backtracking line search to guarantee that our step satisfies the original constraint.
